Four weeks, four money conversations.

A Financial Literacy Month plan for primary and junior classrooms, with free lessons, talk prompts and a 20-minute activity.

A Grade 2 student counts out coins for a pretend snack and gets the right total. Ask why they chose that snack over the other one, and the room gets quiet. The counting was the easy part. The conversation is where the learning about money starts.

November is Financial Literacy Month in Canada, and this year’s national theme is “Talk Money.” For younger students, that theme is good news: a short, well-chosen conversation is something every classroom can do this month.

This article offers our planning suggestions for Ontario classrooms. The national campaign is aimed at Canadians of all ages and is not a school program. Our plan is not endorsed by the Ministry of Education or the Financial Consumer Agency of Canada.

“Talk Money” fits a classroom better than it first appears.

The Financial Consumer Agency of Canada describes this year’s campaign as an invitation to have purposeful conversations about money, because talking helps people weigh options, learn from others and decide with more confidence. Its examples are adult ones, such as emergency funds and debt. See the Financial Literacy Month page.

The idea underneath travels well to a carpet or a table group. A child who can say “I picked this one because we would still have two dollars left” is practising the same habit: putting a choice into words so someone else can respond to it.

Ontario already asks for more than counting.

Financial literacy is its own strand in Ontario’s elementary mathematics curriculum. In the primary grades, the overall expectation centres on understanding the value and use of Canadian currency. From Grade 4, it shifts to the knowledge and skills needed to make informed financial decisions, and students meet ideas such as ways to pay, the relationship between spending and saving, simple budgets and comparing value. See the Ontario curriculum overview for the expectations in your grade.

That shift from “how much” to “which, and why” is where talk earns its place. A calculation shows whether a student can find the total. An explanation shows what they think the total means.

Three questions that open a conversation.

You do not need a new unit to talk money. Three questions, used all month, do most of the work.

“How do you know?”
Asks for the calculation in the student’s own words. Accept counters, drawings, pointing or speech. The goal is a reason someone else can follow.
“What else could we do?”
Makes room for a second option, including not buying anything. Many money tasks have more than one sensible answer, and students notice when a teacher treats them that way.
“What would change your mind?”
Introduces the idea that a good plan can change when the situation does. A price goes up, a need appears, a friend offers to lend something.

A four-week plan using free lessons.

Each week pairs one primary and one junior lesson from our Teacher Hub. Every lesson has a teaching plan, a two-question worksheet and an answer key, takes about 20 minutes, uses pretend money and needs no student account. Add the talk prompt at the end.

Week 1: Count and notice.

Grades 1–3: Dino’s pretend shop, counting, adding and finding what is left. Grades 4–6: Captain Jack compares prices, comparing equal quantities using unit prices. Talk prompt: “How do you know?”

Week 2: Choose.

Grades 1–3: Sammy’s spending choices, comparing a price with a small budget. Grades 4–6: Plan a class celebration, building a balanced budget and comparing trade-offs. Talk prompt: “What else could we do?”

Week 3: Save and wait.

Grades 1–3: Mira’s saving jar, adding to savings and comparing with a goal. Grades 4–6: A six-month adventure, connecting a monthly saving plan with a goal. Talk prompt: “What would you be willing to wait for?”

Week 4: Care for what we have.

Grades 1–3: Captain Jack’s reuse box, comparing what is available with buying something new. Grades 4–6: Repair, reuse or replace?, comparing options while considering safety and usefulness. Talk prompt: “What would change your mind?”

A clear note on fit

These lessons are labelled Grades 1–3 and 4–6 and are starting points to adapt. They are not a Ministry-certified course, and one lesson a week will not cover every expectation in the strand. Use them to open the conversation, then connect to your own long-range plans.

A 20-minute activity for any week.

You can teach this original task with a board and counters. No app or account is needed.

The class shelf / All amounts are pretend dollars

Ten dollars. Three ideas.

Your class has $10 of pretend money for the classroom shelf.

  • Idea A: a set of stickers for $6.
  • Idea B: seeds for a class plant for $4.
  • Idea C: keep the $10 and put it toward a $15 class book next month.

The class may pick one idea or more than one. What would you choose, and why?

  1. Notice and count · 5 minutes. Students find what is left after each idea. Grades 1–3 can model with ten counters.
  2. Choose and explain · 6 minutes. Pairs agree on a plan and give one reason. Ask: “How do you know you have enough?”
  3. Change one thing · 5 minutes. The seeds now cost $5. Does anyone want to change their plan?
  4. Share · 4 minutes. Invite two different plans. Ask: “What changed your mind?” or “Why did you keep your plan?”
Teacher calculation notes

At the start, A leaves $4, B leaves $6, and A and B together cost $10 and leave $0. C leaves $10, which is $5 short of the $15 book. After the change, B costs $5 and leaves $5; A and B together cost $11, which is $1 more than the class has. More than one plan can be justified, including buying nothing this month.

Stretch it for junior grades.

Ask Grades 4–6 to plan across two months: if the class receives another $10 next month, which combinations still allow the $15 book? Students can record their plan as a simple table and explain one trade-off.

Talking money without asking about home.

Families have different resources, and a child should never need to share private details to take part. Give every student the same pretend information, include a choice that costs nothing, and keep real incomes, allowances and purchases out of the task.

If you would like to carry the conversation home, send a prompt with no numbers in it: “Ask someone at home about something they waited for.” It invites a story and leaves the amounts where they belong.
