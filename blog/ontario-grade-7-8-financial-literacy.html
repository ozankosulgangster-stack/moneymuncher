Teaching money takes more than a worksheet.

The real classroom challenges behind financial literacy—and how MoneyMuncher can help teachers make room for better money conversations.

By MoneyMuncher · October 20, 2026 · 7 minute read

In this article

A student can calculate a discount and still struggle to explain whether the purchase is a good choice. Another can build a budget on paper but feel uncomfortable when a lesson asks what their family earns. For a Grade 7 or 8 teacher, teaching money means working with both the mathematics and the lives around it.

Public conversations often ask schools to “teach children about money.” Ontario already includes financial literacy in elementary mathematics. The more useful question is: what helps educators turn those expectations into learning students can use?

This article offers our analysis of classroom planning challenges, informed by Ontario curriculum and Canadian financial literacy guidance. It is not a survey of Ontario teachers or a claim that every classroom has the same needs.

Ontario’s expectations go beyond counting change.

In Grade 7, students encounter currency conversion, financial information, budgets, influences on decisions, interest and account or loan comparisons. Grade 8 adds longer-term planning with tax considerations, balancing priorities, digital explorations of simple and compound interest, and evaluating consumer value. See the Ontario curriculum overview and the Ministry’s Grades 7–8–9 alignment chart.

That asks teachers to connect percentages and calculations with judgement: Which information can we trust? What makes a plan realistic? What should change when the situation changes?

Five classroom challenges worth designing around.

1. Finding a useful starting point takes preparation.

A money task needs plausible prices, clear assumptions and room for discussion. A worksheet can practise arithmetic; a strong lesson also asks students to defend a decision. A short fictional scenario with three choices can make that planning more manageable.

2. Students bring different experiences of money.

Allowance, banking, shopping and saving are not shared experiences. Asking students to compare real family incomes or savings can create pressure. Give everyone the same fictional information, include a no-purchase option and keep home finances private. A budget should open a conversation, not invite comparisons between families.

3. Getting the answer is different from explaining the choice.

The cheapest unit price may require buying more than a class needs. A sale may still exceed the budget. Ask students to show the calculation, explain what matters and name information they would need before deciding. The explanation gives a teacher more to work with than a score alone.

4. One task needs several ways in.

Reading demands, confidence with decimals and communication preferences can all affect participation. Read a scenario aloud, offer a calculation grid, allow calculators when appropriate, and accept spoken, drawn or written reasoning. Keep the decision interesting while adjusting the support.

5. Technology needs to earn its place.

Shared devices, setup time and school approval processes matter. Start with a projected prompt or paper cards when that suits the class. The tool should support teacher judgement and conversation. It should also make clear what is free, what requires a subscription and what is still being piloted.

A 30-minute activity for your next money lesson.

You can teach this original discussion task with a board, paper and an optional calculator. No app or account is needed.

The class showcase / All amounts are fictional CAD

One budget. Three possible plans.

Your class has $100 for showcase materials. Keep $20 as a reserve. All listed prices include any applicable tax.

  • Plan A: buy 8 new display kits at $9 each.
  • Plan B: buy 4 new kits at $9 each and reuse 4 suitable kits already available.
  • Plan C: reuse 8 suitable kits already available, at $0.

Assume reused kits are safe, accessible and suitable. Every plan provides eight kits. Which plan would you recommend, and what else would you want to know?

  1. Notice and calculate · 5 minutes. Set aside the reserve, leaving $80 available. Students work out each plan’s cost and unspent money.
  2. Choose and explain · 8 minutes. Pairs recommend a plan using a calculation and a reason. Discuss condition, usefulness and waste alongside price.
  3. Change one condition · 7 minutes. New kits now cost $11 each. Recalculate and consider whether to revise the choice.
  4. Compare reasoning · 7 minutes. Invite different defensible answers. Ask: “What changed your thinking?” and “What assumption matters most?”
  5. Exit prompt · 3 minutes. “My recommendation is __ because __. One thing I would check is __.”
Teacher calculation notes

Initially, A costs $72, leaving $8 available plus the $20 reserve; B costs $36, leaving $44 available plus the reserve; C costs $0, leaving $80 available plus the reserve. After the price change, A costs $88 and exceeds the $80 spending limit by $8. B costs $44, leaving $36 available plus the reserve. C stays at $0. “Unspent total” includes the reserve; “available money” excludes it. More than one recommendation can be justified.

Build the intermediate extension yourself.

Grade 7: ask students to present the budget before and after the change, then explain how a peer’s preference or an advertisement might influence the recommendation. This offers a connection to budgeting and influences on decisions.

Grade 8: turn the showcase into a longer-term fictional goal. Supply income, expense and tax assumptions and ask learners to revise the timeline. In a separate spreadsheet task, compare simple and compound interest using clearly labelled invented rates. These are teacher-created extensions; the starter activity alone does not cover those expectations.

Financial literacy is a shared responsibility.

Teachers need practical support, and families should not have to disclose private finances for a child to participate. Community groups and educational tools can help by making explanations clear, offering different ways to learn and creating space to practise.

That approach is consistent with the Financial Consumer Agency of Canada’s 2021–2026 National Financial Literacy Strategy, which calls for understandable communication, support for diverse needs and fewer barriers to participation. Learning money skills also does not remove the constraints of income, access or circumstances.

MoneyMuncher’s contribution is to make a thoughtful money conversation easier to start: a fictional choice, an explanation, and a chance to reconsider. For a Grade 7 or 8 class, that can be a useful bridge between doing the calculation and understanding the decision.
