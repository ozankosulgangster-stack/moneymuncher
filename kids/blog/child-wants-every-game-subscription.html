Parent guide · Games and subscriptions

My child wants every game or subscription they see.

You do not need a new argument—or an instant yes or no—every time an exciting offer appears. Give every request the same calm path: pause, price, plan.

September 3, 2026 7-minute read

Use this in the moment

The twenty-second parent script

“I can see why you want it. We do not decide while an ad or game is asking us. Let’s put it on the list, check the real price, and decide on review day.”

01

Wanting it does not make your child greedy

Games make new characters, passes, upgrades, and limited offers easy to notice. Children also hear friends talk about what they play. In that moment, the most exciting benefit is obvious while the trade-off—money, waiting, or choosing one thing instead of another—is harder to see.

Start by separating the feeling from the purchase. Your child is allowed to want something. Wanting is not the same as buying, and “not today” is not a punishment.

Try: “You are allowed to really want it. My job is to help us decide, not to make the wanting disappear.”

One routine for every request

Pause. Price. Plan.

⏸

Pause

Move the decision out of the exciting moment.

Add the item to a family wish list. Review it after a cooling-off period that fits your child—tomorrow, on Friday, or after seven days. The rule works best when it applies to every non-essential digital purchase, not only the requests you dislike.

🔎

Price

Translate the offer into real money.

Check whether the price is one-time or recurring. Convert coins, gems, and points back into dollars. For a monthly subscription, look at the monthly cost and the cost across a full year.

🧭

Plan

Name what this choice replaces.

Ask which money bucket would pay: the child’s digital-fun budget, gift money, or the family entertainment budget. Then name what will wait if this gets the money. A trade-off is clearer than “because I said so.”

02

The five-question game and subscription check

Use the same questions on review day. A younger child can answer them aloud; an older child can look up the details with you.

  1. What does it really cost?Include tax, in-app currency, paid add-ons, and the full cost of a recurring plan.
  2. Is it a purchase or a subscription?If it renews, note the renewal date and how cancellation works.
  3. What would you actually do with it?“Everyone has it” is a feeling. Name the game mode, feature, or activity you expect to use.
  4. Do we already own something that does the same job?Compare it with current games, a library option, or a free version.
  5. What are we willing to wait for instead?Every yes uses money that cannot do another job.

For a free trial, put the end date in the family calendar before starting it. “Free today” and “free overall” are different.

03

Give digital fun a visible limit

A small, predictable digital-fun budget turns you from the permanent gatekeeper into a guide. The amount can be zero, five dollars, or whatever fits your family. What matters is that the rule is clear before the next request arrives.

Example

Your child has $10 for digital fun this month. A $7 game leaves $3. A $6 monthly subscription might fit today, but it needs another $6 next month. A $12 bundle means waiting or saving.

Let unused money roll over if you want saving to become a real option. When children can keep what they do not spend, waiting has a visible benefit.

04

When “not today” brings a big reaction

Do not reopen the whole decision while emotions are high. Keep the response short, acknowledge the disappointment, and repeat the next step.

“I believe that you really want it.”
“The answer is not today.”
“It is on the list. We will look at it on Friday.”

You do not have to persuade your child to like the boundary. Calm repetition makes the process predictable. Return to the money lesson later, when everyone can think again.

05

Four traps for grown-ups

  • Turning every request into a lecture. Use the short script and save the teaching for review day.
  • Buying something to end the argument. That can accidentally teach that asking longer changes the rule.
  • Saying “we can’t afford it” when you mean “we’re not choosing it.” Honest language makes family priorities easier to understand.
  • Using controls without teaching the rule. A password can stop a purchase; it cannot explain recurring cost or trade-offs.

Put this on the fridge

Our family’s game-buying rule

  1. We never decide inside an ad or countdown.
  2. We add the request to our wish list.
  3. We check the price in real dollars.
  4. We wait until review day.
  5. We choose what the money will not buy instead.

06

Back up the family rule with device controls

Purchase approval settings create useful space between wanting and buying. They work best as a safety net alongside the family routine.

iPhone and iPad

Apple’s Ask to Buy can send eligible download and purchase requests to a parent or guardian. Screen Time can also restrict installing apps and making in-app purchases.

Apple: set up parental controls

Android and Google Play

Google Family Link can require approval for selected Google Play downloads and purchases. Google notes that approvals apply to purchases made through Google Play’s billing system.

Google Play: purchase approvals

Menus and eligibility can change by device, age, country, and purchase type. Check the linked platform instructions for current details.
